Re: Save game corruption on Kickstarter games?

by Rastar » 17 Dec 2013, 17:30

I have no experience in professional game development, only "ordinary" enterprise software development. In my time, I haven't seen a single project that was primarily (or even very strongly) driven by quality, only time and budget. People tag releases as "alpha" because they know (or at least strongly suspect) there are still some bugs in it. If they assumed it to be bug-free, management would either tell them to "release today" or "add feature XY". But mostly, project plans and timelines are so, ehh, optimistic, that you won't get into that situation and be happy if you're done *somehow* when the deployment date comes...

But in my opinion, it's a good thing to release early, even if it's not ready, to get feedback as early as possible. If you know there are bugs in it, flag it as alpha and warn them - that way they can decide if the release is worth the hassle. After all, a bug-free product isn't worth anything if it doesn't meet your customers' demands, and you usually only find that out by letting them use it.

Re: Save game corruption on Kickstarter games?

by Rastar » 18 Dec 2013, 03:49

@Mack: I know what you mean, I fully understand your preference. I have worked in quality assurance for a while, and I don't know how many times I told "this isn't ready to ship" but the business side overruled that because "we must ship otherwise the project will be canceled", "they're going to sue us" or something like that...

I guess the settings for our projects are somewhat different. We don't release to the general public, but either to a countable number of customers (if working on a product) or to a single customer (if working on a project). Here you can better manage the expectations for the contents of an alpha release. Also, what usually kills those projects isn't the number of bugs but rather functionality that doesn't meet the needs of your customer. We usually don't come up with an idea that we then implement, but rather have a customer with specific requirements. Or rather, we implement what we think the customer intended to tell us about what he thinks he needs... You can write tons of requirements documents and UML diagrams, but the only way to really find out what's needed and best is "release early, release often". That's what much of the agile process is about, and that's what I meant by "release even if it's not ready". It's often better to find out if you're actually doing the right thing before you polish the code to perfection.

Now, I am very happy with Eric's approach to his product releases. I don't know about budget, but he is definitely putting quality before time, and I think we all benefit from that. But notice that he doesn't commit to release dates and also is somewhat conservative about the feature set in a release. In most enterprise projects you can't do that - they throw a rough requirements document at you and say "now tell me exactly when you're done with it, and how much it's going to cost". And more often than not will fall for the provider that lies best to them about it, so everybody is playing this game. They want their Gantt charts and classical project management plans, although that doesn't work well in software development. Ironically, they know it doesn't work. Nobody will plan to use your software on day one after its release - they will keep a buffer for "they won't finish in time, and then there's one or two things they'll have to redo because they're wrong, oh, and save some time for the bug fixing".

Sorry for this longish rant, but I have been working in this environment for more than a decade, and it seemingly can be a little frustrating from time to time...
