by jwatte » 12 Jul 2007, 08:41

It's not the programmers who have these "happy ideas": it's the owner of the company


Ah, that's why you need the Planning Game!

Once you've been through a few iterations, you know how much task work you'll get done in an iteration. That's when the big boss can come to the table. He's given a list of all the features, with an estimate of how big those features are in "work units." He can pick and choose any features (modulo dependencies, of course), as long as the "work units" don't add up to more than the total for a sprint.

Then you implement the features he chooses, during the sprint. He doesn't get to change his mind until the sprint is done, which is usually one to four weeks from the sprint start. It's important to keep sprints short, to let the big boss change his mind often. If he knows he can introduce arbitrary new features every two weeks, say, he can usually stay away from interrupting the day-to-day work. The only interrupt that's accepted is a total re-set of the sprint, where all work is thrown away, and the project is moved back to the state it was in at the beginning of the sprint. It's like transactional processing, that way.

Yes, you need a lead who can actually explain this system to the big boss, and who has enough of a spine to stick to his guns. Good phrases are "yes, let's add that great feature request to the backlog" and "I'm sorry, but the time to implement that feature fully doesn't fit in this iteration -- do you want to schedule this pre-requisite task for now, so you can get to that feature you want in some future iteration?"

The cool thing is, the entire list of backlog tasks can change as much as you want -- the big boss can re-sort it every day -- but engineering gets to deliver a well-defined task in a reasonable amount of time, using estimates that engineers themselves have made.

Re: Making a MMORPG?

by jwatte » 15 Aug 2007, 00:41

Persistent worlds are their own animal with their own gameplay and own infrastructure needs.


I disagree somewhat. Persistent worlds still need 3D rendering, and user interfaces, and sound playback, and a usable control scheme. All that is shared with a single-player game. PWs also need user chat and movement and networked physics with interactions, which is shared with multi-player games. PWs then add another layer of complexity in that they persist (well, duh!), and they typically add user-modifiable environments and content. Once you have user-modified content and worlds, a lot of the optimizations from the single-player games suddenly become less useful. However, everything you learn while making that single-player game, and then that multi-player game, will help you make better design decisions for a PW. If a PW is the first game you ever write, then the PW part of it may be great, but the underlying game systems will not be robust, and the entire product will suffer for it.
