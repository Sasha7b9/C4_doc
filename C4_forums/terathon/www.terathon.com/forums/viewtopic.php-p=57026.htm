I noticed that. Is C4 headed for a blind alley like the Leadwerks engine or FPS Creator DX10 where it will only run all these fantastic features on a single graphics card with just the right driver for Pixel Shaders 4.2 or something similar? Because that wasn't the case when I originally purchased C4. It's great attraction was that it was going to be an excellent lighting and shadow engine that would run on lower end cards which makes it extremely attractive to indie game authors.

The thing is that the light and shadows were fantastic two years ago and they would run on nearly any Intel graphics chips including portables. I got a refund for Leadwerks engine a while back and frankly it's starting to look like a good idea for C4, for what is misrepresentation and changing requirements that make it unfit for purpose as originally stated.

Incidentally, the Unity engine supports all these things many times over and then some - and it runs on what many consider really old "outdated" graphics cards. Same goes for the Neoaxis engine, which I've been having great success building a game on.

Talk about mountains out of molehills. If anything more ATI support should be dropped to save on wasted dev time due to its general shodiness. No one's going to develop a next gen game for entry level 2000 hardware any more than anyone expects to buy a game and have it work on a ten year old graphics card.

Harry Tuttle wrote:I noticed that. Is C4 headed for a blind alley like the Leadwerks engine or FPS Creator DX10 where it will only run all these fantastic features on a single graphics card with just the right driver for Pixel Shaders 4.2 or something similar?

C4 works on a vast array of hardware, much more than should be needed for any next gen game. Is a 5200gx nvidea or 9200 ATI mobile really going to have enough power to run a game anyone would be making for modern day release? It's ridiculous now, let alone now + development time of x years. If you use OpenGL, the sad fact is also that ATI sucks in their support for OpenGL so that's just the way life is. If ATI starts to do more than the bare minimum for hardware and supports some of their drivers some day it might be worth buying one for a gamer, but as it stands developers ask people to use nviedea for a reason; their stuff actually works. If you can find a production ready game engine that costs under 50k that supports more hardware than C4 and has even vaguely comparable features and performance I'll be very surprised. In fact, I know you won't because no such engine exists. TGEA is the closest thing and it is extremely limited, with outdated scene handling and texture handling that keep high end cards from being fully utilized, not to mention lack of features and general crapulence.

Even if such an engine exists (and I know it doesn't) it's ten times harder to produce low fidelity 3D artwork that looks good. Just look at any torque pack and see how bad the art is by today's standards. If you limit yourself to 1500 poly characters with 256k maps then you are going to spend vastly more time (and money) producing it than if you relax your budget, and it's going to look just as bad on high end graphics machines as it does on the low end cards. If anything, in reality an indie developer is going to wind up with higher polycounts andlarger textures simply due to time constraints, especially if using amateur artists.

Even I can make a hand model that's 500-1000 polies and looks sublime without too much time, but it would be a different story to make a 100 poly hand model that looks even half as good. Even with twice the time.

Harry Tuttle wrote:Because that wasn't the case when I originally purchased C4. It's great attraction was that it was going to be an excellent lighting and shadow engine that would run on lower end cards which makes it extremely attractive to indie game authors.

It's not the case now and it seems in three years the min specs have been virtually unchanged. Real time has advanced 3 years but support levels have moved forward about one year in that time, and only with substandard ATI cards with virtually abandoned drivers.

Harry Tuttle wrote:The thing is that the light and shadows were fantastic two years ago and they would run on nearly any Intel graphics chips including portables. In fact, lights and shadows aside, the entire engine is starting to look really outdated because it did not produce even a subset of the promised features that are considered basic elements for almost three years after it's introduction.


What is C4 supposedly lacking when it comes to graphics? Anything that isn't esoteric and/or only works on the highest end graphics cards? There's lots of bullet points you could throw in to C4 when it comes to graphics, but how many of them both make a real contribution and work on mainstream hardware well? More dynamic shadow map support is the only one that really springs forward. HDR, radiosity, penumbral shadows and specular color maps might be nice but in reality how many machines have the power to make use of these yet?

In case you haven't noticed, the performance for most of the indie price range engines out there is complete crap compared to the bigger engines, especially for the ones with loads of features. Not all of them are particularly stable, either, and some are a nightmare to use and/or have hardly any support or tools. C4 is a huge cut above the competition when it comes to stability and performance, and most especially support.


As for Ogre, Ogre is just a renderer. Of course it has lots of bells and whistles, but the fact is that most of them are not practical for an indy developer to make use of.

In C4 the shaders are built for you and there's a wide variety of tools, tools for most everything you could need for general game making. In Ogre, not only are the tools completely lacking, but you'll also find that using man of its features is going to be a lot more work than you think and it's not necessarily going to look at all like you would expect or want it to without tons of work. If you use Ogre you'll either have to make a materials or shader system of your own or else spend a ton of time directly hacking on shader programming language. You could easily waste years on the latter, and the former will take months and you still won't be done, you'll just have the right tools for the job.

On paper, Ogre is AAA+, but in reality unless you have ten years to spare or a large team of programmers you'll never get half of the features integrated into a game. Even dedicated game engines built on Ogre don't have most of the features you see in its bullet points. There's a huge difference between 'shader support' and a practical system for making a real game with shaders, for example. There's virtually no chance for a one programmer team to make a full game with Ogre that really harnesses its power. If there was, you'd see all kinds of AAA Ogre games out there.

With C4, you have a whole graphics solution. The programmer hardly has to even know or care about graphics to get stunning results. I don't know much and care less, but I have not had much trouble with it. As far as looks the only things lacking in C4 are either esoteric stuff of questionable value or stuff that currently only runs well on very high end cards. With Ogre even once you have all your tools in place, general + game specific, you still need someone who knows Ogre in and out just doing graphics programming.

And if you have someone like that, why even bother with someone else's convoluted renderer in the first place? The biggest advantage is simply help (such as it is) and standardization and not having to deal with driver bugs and quirks quite so much. Otherwise, you're really not getting much above just doing it on your own. Remember, most all these graphics bullet points are supported in the cards themselves. However, efficiently making use of the cards' features is no trivial matter.

By some definitions it's very stable, but again in practice it's pretty finicky and takes a lot of knowledge of its quirks to get anything out of it and while there's some very knowledgable people on the forums the average response is STFU AND RTFM, N00B! On a more sane forum some of the moderators themselves would be banned for acting like tools. Suggestions are also not taken very well, as is typical with open source projects. You will almost invariably get a response of 'do it yourself!' or 'Ogre isn't a game engine!'.

Harry Tuttle wrote:I got a refund for Leadwerks engine a while back and frankly it's starting to look like a good idea for C4, for what is misrepresentation and changing requirements that make it unfit for purpose as originally stated.

Ogre not only not a game engine but it is just a tool for making tools, not something you magically plug in. With C4 you work with the engine source code, in Unity you script a black box. For some that is ideal, for others not.
