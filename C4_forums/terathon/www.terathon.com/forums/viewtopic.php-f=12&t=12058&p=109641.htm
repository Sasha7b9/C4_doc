talondir wrote:Does C4 allow for 3rd person MMO-style game play? more specifically can you use C4 in order to make a traditional MMO?


Yes, the C4 Engine is designed to be very general, and you can make any type of 3D game with it, 3rd person or 1st person included.

talondir wrote:secondly, how much C++ does one really need to know?


To make a game with C4, you would need to be at least moderately proficient in writing C++ programs. The engine does include many examples of how to do things (although some stuff in the demo is more complex than most people will need), and a lot of people have found the consistent and straightforward code in the engine/demo helpful for learning to program in C++.

Re: Pre-purchase Question

by TheRhinoDude » 19 Jul 2012, 15:09

C4 can definately be made to build a traditional MMO, but you will either need to build or buy some additional components.

You said "massive zones", but you didn't state anything about transitions between them. C4 doesn't, today, have the ability to seamlessly load and unload zone maps in the background, but others have added this to the engine, and it is in the C4 roadmap. However, you can do traditional "loading screen" zones easily. And by the time you get your game very far (assuming you are working on your own), I'm quite confident C4 will have this natively.

C4 networking at the raw layer has what is there to support MMO networking in much of the same way you can saw working directly with sockets allows you to build a MMO: it needs more functionality added on top of it. Whether you build or buy this functionality will depend on the needs of your game. The networking built into the demo game is not appropriate for a Internet-deployed multizone MMO with 200 to 300 players per zone. It just doens't scale to that level because it is architected more for accurate physics of a FPS battle of 12 to 16 players, maybe as high as 32 to 64 on a LAN. As such, it does not have client-side prediction. This is not a knock on the demo game neworking, it just is what it is, and is not suitable for MMO zone with 200 to 300 players. If all you need is 50 players, you may be able to get by if you don't have a lot of projectiles.

C4 provies none of the high level stuff which really is the "meat and potatos" for an MMO such as reg/login, sessions, patcing, chat, inventory system, npcs / mobs, loot, experience, quests, combat, raids, merchants / traders / economy, and not to mention databases to store all of this world data as well as account/player data, the list goes on and on: C4 provides none of that. You would have to build or better yet buy as much of that as possible.

One upcoming MMO which uses C4 and is approaching open beta is Trazzy's Wind of Luck, which also uses my AMP MMO middleware which will very soon be launched on the marketplace. I'm working with other teams under NDA right now, if you are interested in becoming one PM me. You can learn more info about Wind of Luck and AMP here:

Wind of Luck: viewtopic.php?f=30&t=11957
AMP: viewtopic.php?f=13&t=11971

So the point is you can definately build an MMO with C4. You can build one faster if you use AMP, or SmartFox Server, or any of the other servers that are out there. That will save you gobs of development time. Plus, like C4, new features are added all of the time, which can further reduce development time.

As to your C++ question, if you want to learn C++ and are just starting out, then C4 is a great api to be playing with and if this if your first C++ project I think this gives a large plus to C4 for your choice. Frankly I can't think of a better one to learn with as it will expose you to well designed code and a diciplined framework.

Several things I strongly recommend are:

1) Don't just jump in and start working on your MMO with C4. Take the time to understand the Simple tutorials, and then the demo game. Then go guild a simple game of your own that has nothing to do with your MMO. I know you are excited to jump right in, but if I may be blunt, right nowyou don't have the tools. As you said, you learn by doing. Well, everything in an MMO is so epic, that the failures are epic as well. You can spend man-months on a piece of technology before you realize you did it all wrong, or doesn't fit with the other pices. The problem is you need to know how all of the pieces will fit together up front, and make sure that jives with your game design.

2) Keep it simple. Don't reinvent the wheel just because you can think of a better way to do it. Stick with what is proven. Where you want to innovate is on your art, and your gameplay.

3) If this is just you working, and you are serious about actually finishing a game of this size, use off the shelf solutions wherever possible. Always buy over build. There will be SO MUCH work just providing content that you want to be spending as minimal time working on infrastructure and middleware issues as possible. Disregard if you are just doing this for fun, or as a learning excercise.

4) While you are evaluating all of the pieces you will be using, learn their strenghts and limitations from a technical and architectural standpoint, and THEN design your MMO gameplay to leverage the strengths, and minimize the weaknesses. If there is too much of a disconnect between the two, then either find another piece of tech to use, or change your design. This is a critical iterative process, and the goal here is to eventually arrive at a game design the technology can support BEFORE you begin construction. Yes this takes time, and yes it is a bit backwards from BUFD principle (Bug Up Front Design), but not doing this is the #1 reason why most large projects fail: disenfranchisement.

What invariably happens is you get this grandiose vision of what you want to do up front, and after spending man months trying to make it happen, you finally realize the technology you've chosen just isn't up to the task without massive work or rework. Morale is low at this point, team members quit, as ultimately everybody realizes that they are only 20% of the way to the finish line, and they no longer recognize or like what they are marching towards. Net result? Dead project.

So plan, design, evaluate, plan, design, evaluate, rinse, repeat until you have figured out not only all of the components underneath, but the game design as well. Once you are confident of that it all fits together, supports each other, and that you are leveraging the strengths and minimizing the weaknesses, then and only then go and start building it.

But first, even before that, learn C++ and C4 on a small project, as you don't even have the knowledge toolset to do that planning and evaluation for something as large as an MMO.

This process could easily take you 6 months to a year, but think of all you will learn along the way. Plus, who knows, maybe along the way you'll think of a much cooler and simpler game idea which you can build in months, not years.

And whether you choose to follow this advice or not, good luck!

- Rhino
