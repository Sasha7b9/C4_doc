surround sound and roadmap

by TheRhinoDude » 19 Apr 2009, 17:00

I noticed from this thread that surround sound is planned eventually:

viewtopic.php?f=3&t=6754&p=60261&hilit=surround+sound#p60261

A few questions:

- I didn't see it on the "Official Roadmap" wiki topic. Is 5.1 surround sound support likely a 1.6. 1.7, 1.8 or beyond feature?

- Speaker configuration is not a standard part of Windows as far as I can tell, it is handled by some vendor specific drivers (ie 2.0 vs. 2.1 vs 5.1 vs 7.1, and others such as Quadraphonic (4.0)). Can C4 detect this configuration, or will it have to expose API's so that the engine can be told what the speaker configuration is?

- From the thread above, it was mentioned that surround will be "automatic". This is fantastic for sound effects. This way I can simply have all of my effects be mono, and let the engine get the directional stuff all for me. Will there also be an option so that multichannel audio tracks can be provided as inputs? This would be extremely useful for the games score (background music), but of virtually zero use for sound effects which are almost always positional.

- in the cases where MCA is not provided, how is bass going to be handled for the .1 (bass channel)? Will a software low-pass crossover be used which samples all currently playing sounds to derive the bass? Bass is inherently non-directional, and therefore the relative position of the sound won't make much a difference (overall distance obviously matters, but not so much direction it's coming from). You typically can't tell if bass is coming from the left or the right. If you can, your either abnormal , or the bass is > 100Hz. Thus the need for the software crossover, otherwise the woofer driver will emit higher frequencies as well which will destroy the soundstage and make what is supposed to be a transparent sound source identifiable.

- can we have programmatic control over the low-band crossover for the bass channel, assuming real-time attenuation is done? The bass channel in a multichannel would be unaffected by this setting, as those would not be sampled. Again, this could then be a user setting, and the user could specify the frequency based on the known capabiltiies of the subwoofer driver.

- how will occlusion be handled wth bass channel? In the real world, the lower the frequency of the bass, the less is it occluded by lesser substantial obstructions. if the bass channel is being sampled already, there may be an opportunity to implement the occlusions so that the lower frequencies are less occluded than the higher frequencies, thereby providing a more realistic simulation. If we have control over the dampening factor of a given material, the combination of the two could be quite effective.

As you can see, I really envision surround sound as several different but related features, and not necessarily all would have to be implemented at the same time:

1) allowing the user to specify their speaker configuration if it can not be accurately determined automatically at runtime
2) taking existing positional sounds in the scene graph and making them play through surround channels if they exist. I would expect this to work for 4 channel (side surrounds), 5 channel (front center), 6 channel (front center + rear center), or 7 channel (side + rear surrounds). A sound source whizzing around the player in a circular pattern should seamlessly move between these drivers, utilizing every one. I don't think the solution should assume only 2 surrounds. In my house alone I have 2.0, 2.1, 5.1, and 7.1 configurations between my PC's and HTPC's, and I game on all of them.
3) play 'well' through the subwoofer, attenuating the mids and highs appropriately. If this can't be done inexpensively, make it an option so that users can choose between: a) ignore subwoofer for spatial effects (cheapest), b) play full spectrum for subwoofer spatial effects (mid-range), and c) attenuate spatial effects for subwoofer (best sounding, most expensive).
4) support multichannel audio file formats. These either would not be settable at the high level within the scene graph, or if they were the channels would have to be ignored or merged in a resampling (note: does this happen today with stereo?) Where this would be extremely awesome is for playing background music. This can make a *huge* difference in how immersive a game is.

Regarding the first question (roadmap), from my perspective, all of the planned terrain features and foliage system are higher priorities than surround sound. The same for the unified gui / panel editor. Surround is important...but by no means critical. Most gamers don't have surround rigs anyway. For me, it would fit somewhere in 1.8.

Thanks in advance for answering these!

Re: surround sound and roadmap

by Eric Lengyel » 19 Apr 2009, 17:22

We're definitely planning to enhance the sound experience in C4, but it will come after the stuff currently on the roadmap.

Currently, mono sounds that are positioned in 3D space get mixed into the left and right channels as appropriate for their positions. When more channels are added, the mixing will use all of them correctly.

Stereo music currently plays to the left and right speakers as you would expect. We do plan to allow multi-channel playback when explicit support for 5.1+ is implemented.

If the engine can determine the speaker configuration, it will use it. We'll most likely provide some kind of dialog setting where the user can specify the config (or override the default).

As for the bass channel, we would definitely have it take low-frequency data from all of the surround channels. We have not settled on any kind of external API for controlling that yet, but we'll take your suggestions into consideration.

Re: surround sound and roadmap

by TheRhinoDude » 19 Apr 2009, 23:07

Thanks for the prompt reply Eric...I hope you weren't working on a Sunday.

I agree that this is not nearly as important as almost everything on the roadmap. There are only 1-2 line items in 1.8 that I would rank, at best, as equal. Everything else is clearly more important.

That is great to hear that you are planning on specific support for MCA playback, that is huge for the stuff I do. Just a warning though...it does open up quite a rats nest of issues which I'll get to below.

First a followup question:

- What happens currently when a stereo sound effect (or music) is played in 3D space, assuming it is allowed? Does it get resampled as mono and then positioned normally? Is a channel discarded? Obviously this is probably a bad idea for 99% of games (the engineer would instead simply combine the channels ahead of time)...but bear with me.

Getting back to MCA support...

First, I know people have asked for Ogg, but if you are going to support multi-channel, it's probably best you stick with WAV, as it's arguably the best format for one stop shopping (at least on Windows). By adding multi-channel WAV support, you will give *ultimate* flexibility to the content author, as they can provide you files that have *any* combination of 18 channels:

http://www.microsoft.com/whdc/device/au ... chaud.mspx

If you want to support 7.1, you will need to support the WAVEFORMATEXTENSIBLE structure. Standard WAV will only go to 6 channels (5.1). Either way, you can mask which of the 18 channels they define. For example, while 2.1 isn't an official standard, it's something you run into VERY often in the desktop multimedia space. 2.1 is a vastly more common runtime configuration than 5.1. With multichannel WAV, you can support any combination of up to 6 channels. In fact, there's no reason why you can't create a mono wave fil that is LFE only. Totally legit file format.

One of the decisions you'll also have to make is what to do when the game has one or more given multichannel tracks, but there the runtime multichannel configuration does not match. For example, if the game includes 5.1 only, and yet the current config is 4.0. What happens? The reverse is easy: some speakers simply go unused. But what happens when there aren't enough speakers for the available track?

The easily way to handle this would be to push the issue off onto the content developers, and recommend that they provide the different content mixes above 2.0 if desired, and you use the most appropriate (ie highest supported) at runtime. That's easy for you, performs well at runtime, and frankly isn't that bad for audio engineers with decent tools, as most will make this faily straightforward. However, it will bloat the game size, and for developers without the high end tools, it will be a major PITA. Bloat is also an issue with this approach.

Another easy way out is to ditch the unsupported channels, but that will often result in the poor quality. For example, let's assume a 5.1 sound track (the only one shipped) is playing in a 4.0 configuration. Simply discarding the bass and the center channel would often end up with music that sounds tinny, and cavernous with no cohesive soundstage as the playback will have far more channel separation than the original engineer had afforced due to the entire missing center channel. I think this is the worst option, but if the sound engineer desiged for it up front, discarding the center channel may not be a bad thing, and therefore this may be desired runtime behavior.

Another option is to down-mix the provided channels at runtime into the available channels where there is a discrepancy, and theoretically if you end up handling bass for spatial sound effects, you can likely use the exact same code used for that to handle this. This way content developers only ever have to provide a 5.1 or even 7.1 sound track, and the channels would be appropriately mixed at runtime by C4 if there aren't that many speakers in the configuration. In some cases it will be combining two channels into one (ie L = L + 0.5C), in other cases three channels (L = L + 0.5C + 0.5LFE). The standard mixer already used could handle this easily I think.

Therefore I would recommend the following algorithm:

for each multichannel version available of a given resource, highest number of channels to lowest
- if MCA channels are equal to or a subset of available speaker channels, play as is
- if MCA channels are greater than available speaker channels
- consult engine / user setting and either:
- resample the missing channels down to available channels via mixer, or
- discard extra channels, or
- continue down to next best fit

This would necessitate a virtualization of the multichannel wav's. For example, foo_20.wav and foo_71.wav can both be provided, but in code "foo.wav" can be referenced, and the right wav is loaded, based on the setting which says to either:

- resample the closest fit
- use best fit
- discard channels

So in this example where foo_20 and foo_71 are avilalbe, if 4.0 configuration was detected:

- the first option would resample 7.1 -> 4.0
- the second option would simply use 2.0
- the third option would use 7.1 file, but throw away C, LFE, RR, LR

This could even be exposed to the user on the Audio Setup dialog, as long as it's overridable within the game.

Does this seem too complicated?

Of course, none of this is attempting to implement Dolby Pro Logic or Dolby Pro Logic II or Dolby Pro Logic II Ex, or Phantom Center, or any other sampling which will actually turn two channel into multichannel. Fmod can do this, but in my experience, this is worthless for games. Combining channels is a whole lot simpler, and more effective, than trying to create them.

I know this is many months off from implementation, but just throwing out some random thoughts.

Eric Lengyel wrote:
TheRhinoDude wrote:What happens currently when a stereo sound effect (or music) is played in 3D space, assuming it is allowed?

As far as channel matching goes, the plan is to use a pre-defined mixing matrix to distribute extra channels in the source data among the channels that actually exist in hardware.


Just to make sure we are on the same page, the mixing matrix would only mix-down, right? You would never try to play a 2.0 source through a 5.1 setup by extrapolating new channels (ie Dolby Pro Logic, etc.)?

I was thinking about this more, and I think the following solution will give total control and at the same time provide a simple programming model with logical defaults:

1) provide a capabilities API to determine speaker config as detected or specified by the user. This could be as simple as a single function that returns an array of enumerated channel ID's as defined by the 18 different WAV multichannel flags:

Front Left - FL

Front Right - FR

Front Center - FC

Low Frequency - LF

Back Left - BL

Back Right - BR

Front Left of Center - FLC

Front Right of Center - FRC

Back Center - BC

Side Left - SL

Side Right - SR

Top Center - TC

Top Front Left - TFL

Top Front Center - TFC

Top Front Right - TFR

Top Back Left - TBL

Top Back Center - TBC

Top Back Right - TBR

2) individual Sound instances provide a ChannelMatching enum property with possible values of RemixUnavailableChannels (default) vs. IgnoreUnavailableChannels. More could be added later. This property is ignored for mono wav's.

RemixUnavailableChannels uses the "pre-defined mixing matrix" exactly as you described, and is the default.

IgnoreUnavailableChannels simply ignores any channels that can't be mapped to missing speakers.


This lets the programmer provide exactly what the sound engineer wants on a per-track basis. In partcular:

- By consulting the caps api, the developer can leverage a factory pattern and work with the sound engineer to provide multiple tracks where desired. For example, they could provide a 5.1 version and a 2.0 version of the track. The engine wouldn't know anything about this. It would simply be asked to play one of the sounds as the game retreived from the Factory. The Factory class would consult the caps api to determine which one it creates.

- By consulting the caps api, the program can choose whether to ignore or remix based on the actual capabilties. For example, for my 5.1 tracks, I'd want them to remix for quadraphonic (4.0), but ignore for stereo (2.0). This can be baked into the Factory class and coded once, as once the appropriate Sound is constructed, the ChannelMatching property can be set appropriately based on the caps.

- The sound engineer can design his tracks for either ignore, remix, or both depending upon track. We can leverage a simple naming scheme for the file which says which fallback mechanism should be used. Or there could be some XML metadata some place. Either way, the Factory would handle this, and set the property appropriately based on the content metadata.

As you can see, having the simple caps api, combined with the control over of either remix vs. ignore, the developer and sound engine have total control if desired. Conversely, if they don't care as much (or let's say it's early in the dev cycle...so they just don't care *yet*), they can simply provide 5.1 tracks only, and the defaults will suit them fine. Plugging in a singleton Factory later would be a very easy refactor, if they aren't happy with the automatic mixing, on a track by track basis.

What do you think?

cboyce wrote:This doesn't really make sense to me. All sound sources are "mono" in real life. Sound comes from one source, and we hear it in stereo because we have two ears. "Stereo" sound sources don't really make sense to me, unless you're actually simulating a stereo audio system. I don't care what makes sense in real life

All I want is to have nice firing sound with some amount of baked stereo in it + to have subtle echoes.


This is an excellent point. For extremely local sounds, like the firing of a shotgun, the sound engineer is going to be able to produce a much more enthralling sound effect if he can use stereo. Think about it...at least for FPS:

- the sound is eminating from a point usually directly in front of the camera
- the sound is often directly centered on the screen, or close, and doesn't change as the camera moves
- occlusion will rarely if ever come into play due to closeness of the sound and the camera
- however, echo / reverb has to come into play greatly

Playing virtually the same exact same mono sound, eminating from the center of the screen, at close range, directly through both speakers is not going to sound as compelling as a stereo blast. If the spatialization is working correctly, the L and R sound will be nearly identical, as the source of the sound is so close to the camera, and therefore an audio engineer can make a stereo sound effect that sounds much better. However, the only way to get echo is to use a mono sound. You can't have both.

If you think about it, this really is only an issue for sounds that eminate from the close proximity to the camera position, and echo/reverb is really all that comes into play, not occlusion.

So really all that is needed here is that for a stereo Source:

1) it is played back as per normal today, directly as a stereo sound, and it is up to the developer to ensure only mono tracks are used for "true" spatializations of point sounds

and if reverb effect is enabled, either:

2A) the stereo channels are coalesced, the reverb effect is generated from the point of the Source, and this is mixed and played back through *both* channels, with the original source

or

2B) the L and R channels are treated separately and each has reverb effect generated independently and mixed back into the source channel during the stereo playback. This would be more realistic, but more expensive computationally as I assume the second independent reverb calculation, from the same point in space, is far more costly than simply coalescing first into a mono signal and doing the reverb calculations once.

So in short a multichannel Source will act partly as a Sound, and partly as a Source. It will play directly as a Sound, but the channels are also used for reverb calculation (either independently or after being coalesced), which is mixed back into the Sound playing through the channels.

That is probably exactly what Source is doing, or similar. It would give you the best of both worlds. The sound engineer can come up with a shotgun blast that sounds *much* better tha simply hearing (virtually) the same exact mono sound from both speakers, but at the same time you get the dynamic nature of the reverb, so the shotgun blast sounds quite different when firing in the middle of a very open room vs. firing right next to a wall in a small room.

Many reverb algorithms ..even ones that claim to be stereo, tend to work like this. (of course you DO get proper stereo reverb algorithms ... high end Lexicons for one ... and various plugin ones such as in the Waves bundles have become more common these days)

2B) the L and R channels are treated separately and each has reverb effect generated independently and mixed back into the source channel during the stereo playback. This would be more realistic, but more expensive computationally as I assume the second independent reverb calculation, from the same point in space, is far more costly than simply coalescing first into a mono signal and doing the reverb calculations once.


One does not tend to 'directionalize' ambiance in the same way one does a direct sound. At least in stereo. That does tend to change a little when you come to surround sound though ... as a reverb 'bloom' , emanating from the direction of the source and spreading to the opposite side is very effective.

At the end of the day though ... what one has to consider is , is it better to try and perform the very intensive CPU calculations that really good reverb requires, or is it better to record the same sound source in different locations ... or record it dry and create ambiance with 3rd party tools (such as the impulse environments you get in Altiverb for instance), and use a static recorded track for the ambiance instead ?

It obviously depends on the situation. But for something like a gun shot, I think I would prefer to go with prerecorded ambiance. When you are dealing with very high SPL sounds, it is difficult to get a very genuine *effect* due to a number of factors, not least of which is the way the ear reacts to sudden increases in pressure levels, which you can't realistically reproduce without sending speaker cones into the walls :p .... so you need to start factoring in other processing effects ... it might just be easier to create a recording which 'sounds right' by doing your processing off line first.

by Twisted Helix » 22 Jun 2009, 04:49

Actually they have the most distinct early reflections. Which is what the ear/brain uses to determine distance from objects and size of space.

It is worth bearing in mind that these film library sounds have incredibly over accentuated reverb tails that sound nothing like a gun does in reality. They do this for two reasons (and the game industry has largely followed their lead). Firstly without the recoil , and other sensory input, developers attempt to make up for that by over accentuating the visual and audio effects. Secondly as it is a kind of tradition from the movies, where they would have you believe that every bullet fired ricochets down a long metal tube.

Out doors , in reality , a gun shot sounds like a sharp crack. Like a whip almost. A shotgun sounds 'poff'. Indoors the initial percussion deafens you so much that you rarely hear early reflections, and usually just the 'ghost' of the reverb tail.

Regards how sounds are used in present games, well in BF2 the first player sounds are all stereo 44k1Hz. The third player sounds are all mono 22k5Hz. For directionality you want a mono sound .... a stereo sound plays back equally from both speakers. Pan it into one speaker and you merely merge the left and right channels. Therefore to avoid any phasing issues, ideally for directional sounds , you want to use mono or one side of a stereo channel.
