A Thought About Lighting Techniques

by DrVonBraun » 26 Oct 2013, 06:09

I am having some trouble with lighting a city level correctly. Maybe the problem comes from the fact that a city is mostly in 90 degree angles and the walls are mostly parallel to each other.

The way this problem manifests is pretty simple. You place some form of infinite light somewhere and let it illuminate the city. Now the faces that are toward the light look more or less ok, the other side doesnt, so you add some more infinite lights to compensate but they illuminate certain areas more than you want them. But the real problem comes with shadows. Houses are huge and they cast huge shadows in narrow streets so that even most of the parts that are towards the light source are dark sometimes. I compensate for that with another lightsource close to the main one but this one casts no shadow. Again, this might work for the shaded areas but now the non shaded areas are too bright sometimes. This is all amplified by the fact that you have huge flat surfaces. With organic bodies it's not so harsh but with huge cubes, it becomes a problem

Ambient light of course causes no shading so it is only of some help here.


I was wondering, what if there was a light type that would automatically illuminate the other side of an object with somewhat of a weaker light. In reality, ambient light is light that is reflected from surfaces, dust particles etc and that light will not cause the form of flat effect that ambient light in a 3D engine does, simply because it illuminates real surfaces with real curvatures whereas ambient light in a 3D engine simply ignores the normals.

So what if there was a special light type that would also illuminate the triangles with normals that point away from the light source, just somewhat dimmer in order to represent the fact that they are on the side that points away from the sun. It could take the different normals into account and create a shading effect on the surface , yet its darker than the side that points toward the light, thus giving a more normal impression. the advantage over using multiple light sources is that you dont have to coordinate them and deal with overexposure in areas that are hit by multiple light sources.

I'm not a 3D expert so perhaps I'm just rambling about something that is already implemented somewhere but at least I havent seen anything like that in the C4 engine. Would this be a realistic idea?

Re: A Thought About Lighting Techniques

by Jesse Meyer » 26 Oct 2013, 06:54

Without Radiosity or some other kind of Image Based Lighting, this kind of situation is a normal and expected by game artists to prepare for and handle. Many games approach it differently, and there's a reason why Pixar lists three dozen "lighting artists" in the credits for their movies.

Configuring your Ambient Light and "Sun" light correctly are critical but not always completely intuitive in C4 just yet. This is mostly due to the lighting not being handled linearly (gamma correct specular). When that is compounded with that a lot of eyes are now trained to expect HDR, artists tend to over-pronounce the lighting and we suffer terrible contrasts and blowouts. This is not an easy problem.

The answer is really part of the same heart that answered your question on visibility determinism.

What I've done is work more cube maps into the environment output which provides the nice shading in the ambient pass you're currently missing out on. The rest is entirely manual, working very hard matching the lighting in my scenes with a photo of some kind in a similar region geographically with the proper weather conditions.

Maybe you could use Half-Lambert lighting instead of classical Lambertian shading (see https://developer.valvesoftware.com/wiki/Half_Lambert). It wraps the light around the object.

You basically do

Code: Select all
float lambert = dot(normal, lightDir) *0.5 + 0.5


I haven't used C4 shaders up to now, so I can't translate that into C4 terms.

I've been running some experiments that involve storing radiosity data in ambient spaces, and it looks promising. This would allow light bounces to be precalculated so that the back sides of buildings would receive light from the lit front sides across the street. Any models moving through the area in between would also receive the bounced light, so it goes beyond what ordinary light mapping could accomplish.

by MACK » 28 Oct 2013, 09:38

(Presuming I understand this correctly)
Then this is a type pre-calculated "Global Illumination".
It will help tremendously to give a more natural look to your rooms and open spaces because it will pre-calculate the light that is supposed to bounce off the walls and objects that you include in the scene during the calculation phase.

Light currently (as you already noticed) does not bounce off walls. So, by precalculating this you can easily give the illusion that it is bouncing off the walls.

Because this technique is precalculated (and I wanted to be sure), then it will not work for moving lights. However, objects that move inside your scene, will be able to receive the light from the lights in the original scene.

Jesse is basically saying that you still have to adjust the lighting to make your scene look "great". But, yea.. Global Illumination will really help quite a lot.
