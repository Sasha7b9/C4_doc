For soft, springy suspension, ray casting seems to work ok for the most part. I haven't found a good solution for more realistic car physics, where you need to simulate much stiffer springs and faster adjustment forces. You can either apply a single impulse force based on the current estimated time until the next Move() call, or you can apply a constant force. Either way, since the physics steps are independent of the Move() calls, they're never perfectly matched and either heavy forces or drastic frame rate differences cause the forces to be too great or too little.

by jwatte » 09 Jul 2012, 00:31

you need to simulate much stiffer springs and faster adjustment forces


Yeah, actually integrating forces in Move() and not doing that at the same rate as solving contacts and constraints is a bummer. I hear Eric is planning an update to joints/physics in the future, though. Perhaps after shipping the 31st?

It's actually okay to simulate even pretty stiff suspensions with forces/torques, if you can apply them at the same step rate that you detect/simulate them. What I've found helps the most is:

1) Keep a low center of gravity. Typically, this means keeping the pivot (node point) of the rigid body at, or close to, the bottom edge of the chassis box.

2) Use a PID controller. For each suspension point on the chassis, calculate the actual world velocity (of the point,) as well as the amount of deflection from ideal. This allows you to apply a force at that point (which turns into force + torque on the rigid body,) and that force + torque can counter-act the velocity of that point, as well as add the sprint force. This works as a shock absorber, pretty much.

If you can simulate at 120 Hz, or even 300 Hz, you can do even better stuff. As you increase time resolution, though, you start creeping donwards in floating point resolution, to the point where you start actually noting floating point quantum effects from cancellations in the math.
