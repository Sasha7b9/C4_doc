by ordaz » 05 Jul 2007, 16:09

Right, I have to *seriously* insist on some wise points already told by some of the wise folks 'round... and I've gone through this stuff myself.

1.- Experience is a must. Not just about coding, designing or artwork: an agile and rock-solid workflow must be fully working before ever dreaming about making MOGs. And note I didn't say "MMOGs". Start making one (or more) single-player game(s). Then, take the LAN route all the way down: prediction, bot handling, user-issued commands, server-side savegames and more. Then have a pit stop at the lounge system and learn to love it: you could expand a 2D chatroom to a 3D meeting place. After that, you're allowed to say "MOGs" aloud.

2.- Design, design, design. Not even a single line of code must be written before a LOW LEVEL Design document is FULLY FINISHED. Breathe. Get a high level design document done, feel comfortable with it, take apart anything that doesn't fit and add as many details you can. Of course, keep it readable and clean, well organized. Define and describe EVERYTHING. Then, write every low-level design document your crew will ever need. Don't underestimate the importance of design!

3.- Art vs. Content. Granted, you'll need huge amounts of 3D, textures and the like, but you'll also need people working on crafting levels/scenarios *exclusively*. Don't try getting programmers or artists making levels as an extra task: have your Level Creation crew working only in crafting levels, this is not negotiable. Your artists can make wall sections, trees, whatever: your level crew will use those "pieces" to build your game world. Your programmers can make a great AI system: your level crew will place the bots/spawning points, triggers, whatever. They have their own territory: they can model, texture, script and even code, but you need them focused on crafting great levels, missions and the like. No multitasking here, really.

4.- "Engine-blending" doesn't fit all. I'd rather use NeL than Torque Network, but both can be painful. And most budgets can't afford $50,000 licenses (per seat) to use fully working MMOG engines (NDAs signed, no names here). So, the tech base is a hard decission which has to be made... by your Lead (or Principal) Programmer: evaluate, test, try, ask the programmers... Geez, this one's tricky. So, when a High Level Design Doc is ready, give it to him (or her) so the programmers can start making their minds on how to face the project. They choose C4, C4+NeL, C4+in-house net code...? Whatever: their decission, their responsibility.

5.- Money. Big bucks. MMOGs just need it. And some numbers are scary: I've warned you. Oh, of course: (time * money) makes even more scary numbers. Even if you're one of those "money is not a problem" buds: money will become a problem, sooner or later. For the skeptical about this: Think about maintenance fees, security, certificates, taxes, credit card stuff...

6.- Play other MOGs. Understand them, their business models, their structure. Guild Wars is not like WoW. Perhaps you can come up with a different, affordable and achiveable new MOG model (well, you can try). No matter what: learn from what others have done (or tried) before.
In the end, this humble post of mine is just about this.

7.- Shield your IP. Register your ideas, whatever. You can always sell or license it if something goes wrong.

8.- Once up and running (congrats if so) there's a long way to go: expand, translate, develop new content, new missions... and add that to the game on a regular basis (monthly, 3 months, you name it). And once you really start making money, don't stop developing, be it a v2.0 or another game. Keep moving! And treat your players well: birthday presents, freebies... Sponsor forums, online communities, conventions... Hopefully, that day will come, but you must be ready for it from beta!

Well, that's about it.
Cheers!

I'm quite busy these days (new job!) but I'm dropping by as I'm now working (cough) on a project which would need at least 110 simultaneous players. No prediction, no persistence, just easy stuff, but I'm trying to get "inspired". So, if I happen to be able to give some clues, I'll try...

by jwatte » 09 Jul 2007, 07:38

Not even a single line of code must be written before a LOW LEVEL Design document is FULLY FINISHED.


I disagree. When you do the Big Design Up Front, you will find, half-way through the project, that what you learned doing the project invalidated at least half of that design. Especially for a small team, Big Design Up Front can be a total project-killer.

I would, instead, start by "growing" the product. Take small steps, and test each step for whether it's good or not. Always keep an eye on resource usage (i e, don't allow your NPC to use 100 milliseconds of thinking time in a single step), but otherwise keep your options open. Write the code that seems best at the time when you deliver a particular feature.

Keep a big list of everything you THINK you'll need to do. After each feature delivery (every week, or so), take another look at that list. Re-order the things on the list; remove what you don't think is going to be needed (or fit), and add whatever you feel you must. Then pick the highest priority item from the list, and dive back into implementing that particular part.

Don't get hung up on too much software structure. Try to stay with some basic building blocks (interfaces, execution environments, and message queues, say) and stay away from globals. Change the structure at the point where you run up against some unsolvable problems. Implement the feature first, then look at how it lies in the code, and if it doesn't fit well (looks like a kludge), re-factor the code until it fits better.

Don't start a task without defining how you're going to know you're done with that task. Typically, this means adding a test case to your ever-growing suite of automated tests. These can be unit tests, functional tests, or other kinds of tests.

Last, front-load the "art pipe" part of the equation. Make sure artists can quickly export data out of the content tools, start putting levels together, and see things animating. Dummy out networking, game logic, or databases to get there. Think through how you'll distribute the state/events/game logic, but don't necessarily implement until you're actually ready. (YAGNI and all that)

And, yes, aiming at "MOG" is better than "MMOG" unless you have a well functioning, stable team that already knows how to work together.

by jwatte » 10 Jul 2007, 07:32

A story from real life:

When we built There we used a traditional, BDUF, design-document driven process. Or, "the story of how you spend $40M and don't get a finished game." Now that we re-focused the company on providing a technology platform, we've been following something like Scrum for development, which is much more agile, and much more aimed at producing measurable improvement in every small iteration. I must say that it's a huge improvement, and would have been for the There project, too.

"happy ideas"


I'm not saying "don't use process control." I'm saying "don't do too much work that you will throw away." In the process I suggest, you create a map of what the next iteration is about, with a stated goal of delivering X feature. Programmers break this into tasks, which are estimated for time requirements. Each day, programmers log time against these tasks, which lets us plot how far we have left until delivering the iteration. You can say that each iteration has a design up front. However, this is not a "big" design -- each iteration covers one through three weeks.

After the iteration is done, you sit down, look at the big list of things you'll want to do, and pick the next thing to do. Typically, you pick the thing that delivers the most immediate value, first, and/or you pick the biggest technology risk, first (the value is then risk reduction). "happy ideas" go onto the big list of things you might want to do, and only gets picked if they bubble up to the top. Programmers spending all their time on "happy ideas" during an iteration (called "sprint" in Scrum, btw), end up not logging any progress against the agreed tasks, and will probably succumb to peer pressure pretty quickly.

Is Scrum a panacea? Not at all. You still need programmers who know what they're doing. You still need to pay attention to process control. You need to have designers who can reliably represent user desires. However, with Scrum, you do reduce the risk of spending a lot of time building things you don't need, because you deliver the most value in each iteration, and if an iteration goes off track, you kill it right then and there, and re-start with a new iteration -- so the most you risk is a week or two of loss.
