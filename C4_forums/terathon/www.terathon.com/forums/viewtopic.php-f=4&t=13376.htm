Hello, and welcome to C4.

The main answer to your question is that it is quite hard but whether or not you can even achieve this depends on your programming ability.
From your post, it sounds like you do have experience with this.
More Specifically, there's probably only a handful of people in this forum that would even attempt to add global illumination, hardware instancing, displacement mapping or tesselation. (I am not aware of anyone who has actually done any of these but would be glad to hear about it.)

However, if you buy a license to the source code, then you could certainly try.
I have seen very difficult tasks like this posed as questions in the forum, and you would probably get a response that such changes would not be recommended and the rendering pipeline could change at any moment. Since this is a very difficult task, you would likley have to learn most of this on your own by carefully analyzing the code. To put it another way, there's 2 books that discuss many different topics on the C4 Engine, and neither of them (AFAIK) discuss how to modify the rendering code in any significant way. Don't let that stop you though if you have the ability. Most of the forum members leave the rendering side to Terathon. (There are a few who have in fact changed some aspects of the renderer).
So, having some others who had this ability would certainly be welcome.


In regards to making a purchase,obviously, you can't make any of these types of changes without a license. I can't give a 100% chance, but historically, there has always been a year end / holiday sale of some kind, so hopefully that will still be there.

As far as overall engine design, to quote from the book, "the C4 Engine source is renowned for its highly
organized architecture and use of professional C++ coding techniques.".

Re: C4 as a learning tool

by aubrey.r.jones » 12 Dec 2013, 10:51

To learn how a high-quality engine might be designed, C4 is an excellent teaching aid. Certainly, it is one of the most consistent and well organized codebases I've ever read. Much better than *any* other game/graphics engine I've worked with. It shows less of the evolutionary technical debt that something like Source or Unreal has, and it has far more focus and coherence than the open-source engines like JME and Ogre.

Because the entire engine is so lean and tightly wound, changing the manner in which it acts is quite difficult. It's relatively easy to hack in new things that match existing engine concepts (eg. adding a new node type, interfacing external physics). But if what you want to do violates the basic assumptions of the engine, you're going to find that it's difficult or impossible to implement your design.

Here's a case in point: at first glance, it seems easy to add hand-written shaders to the system. There's no reason you can't just intercept the call that asks for GLSL from the shader graph, and insert your own code. Except that the shader graph system builds and keeps track of the interface variables between shader stages, as well as what information needs to be fed in (as uniforms and buffers), and which stages output to which buffers--which makes sense, as all that can be formulated as a graph problem anyway. But now your "hand-written shader" has to have text replacement (at minimum) for interface variables, which need to be dug out of the dummy graph that you build to represent your code--so that C4 will connect up appropriate buffers, attributes, etc.

Now, all that's doable. But, dude, it's a *lot* of work. It's going to be complex and fragile. And then the new version of C4 will come out, and all that complex, fragile code you wrote will be broken.

So, what I'd say is that if your interest is writing a rendering system... just write your own, and hook it into appropriate libraries for physics, sound, etc. There's an awful lot that C4 can teach you, and it's a good tool for actually building a game, but hacking it up is a pain in the gonads.
