Re: industrial buildings

by Frank Skilton » 28 May 2009, 23:19

This is a quote from Eric, from the page that JamesH links to:

The real problem with your level is that you have giant geometries. This is bad for a dynamic lighting engine because if any part of a geometry is illuminated by a light source, then the whole geometry has to be rendered. In your level, this means that huge meshes that aren't even on the screen are getting sent through the pipeline just because you're able to see small parts of them. The engine can't efficiently cull objects at a granularity smaller than individual geometries -- it has the send the whole geometry to the graphics chip and let it do per-polygon culling in hardware. This is also killing the performance of the portal system. You really need to divide your level into bite-sized pieces so that the engine can toss out anything that's not on screen or visible through portals. For example, if there's a gadget attached to a wall, it should be a separate geometry node instead of part of the wall mesh. Also, the walls themselves should be divided into pieces instead of having one big mesh for the entire boundary of a room.

Re: industrial buildings

by MachineryFrank » 29 May 2009, 01:38

atlury wrote:Set of several layers of mesh? You mean how? How would that benefit the use dynamic lights? Can you please explain a bit frank. I am a noob in art.


Frank Skilton already linked to a technical explanation. I will try to explain from the modeling view point:
I try to separate my indoor models into pieces. There are corridor parts, door frames, pillars, corridor junctions where several parts meet, piles of dirt, wood boards, crates and so on. They are not one continuous mesh, they are separate layers in my modeling part, similar to separate models.

This way the engine can do better culling. And if your dynamic lights have small enough light ranges then a light affects only the models that are in view. Besides that you have more flexibility to use portals and zones for scene management.
