Re: This looks promising (VR-Headset)

by mschifter » 22 Aug 2012, 13:12

I once had the opportunity to wear a $150,000 Kaiser ElectroOptics headset and helped integrate a physical cockpit f-16 simulator with its own display (actually 3 displays collimated into 1) and a high-end SGI machine rendering the same database as the f-16 simulator.

This was so cool, because the Kaiser display was the equivalent of round sunglasses, and anything in the 3d SGI environment that was black I could see through, and anything else was what was being rendered by the SGI. SO I set up a big black polygonal mask around the pilot in the 3D plane on the SGI, and I could still see and operate the switches in the cockpit (where the virtual polygonal mask was). However, when I looked downwards over the side of the cockpit I could see the ground rushing by. The Kaiser was tracking my head movements as well, so we're talking really cool effect. It's the closest I've ever been to being in a plane.

We didn't sell any that day, but I'll never forget it. That was 1995.

Re: This looks promising (VR-Headset)

by mschifter » 27 Aug 2012, 13:45

1280x720 with full 60 Hz and extremely low latency (< 1 frame) would be sufficient, though.


Visual latency is usually not a problem. Head tracking latency and accuracy are typical issues that have to be overcome for the product to be viable. Usually a predictive smoothing algorithm is applied to the head tracker. If the tracker update rate is too slow, is inaccurate (jitter due to low tracker resolution or position noise from the environment), or otherwise falls out of sync with the visuals perceptually, the result is simulator sickness and a useless product. It doesn't take much to lose sync.

However, if this device will track much faster -- at 120 Hz without jitter or significant latency, it would be a winner alright.

Re: This looks promising (VR-Headset)

by MACK » 28 Aug 2012, 08:23

re: Pixel density not being perceived:

This might be true if the density was greater than the perceivable resolving power of the eye. But that would take a lot of pixels.

For example, The retina display from apple and its ~300 pixels per inch. But in order for the term to be valid... the screen needs to be held away from the eye by a fixed distance of ~1 foot. If you do that, then the screen is perceptibly smaller and does not fill the visual field.

So, in order for a VR screen to be useful it must fool the eye into seeing a screen that is "virtually" bigger... all of the pixels still have to "fill up" the field of view. No matter how you slice and dice it... 800x600 pixels is not going to display more information to your eye when it is very close, than it does when its a few feet away. The only difference is that it get's bigger when its closer.

So, your eye might be able to better visualize the screen, but the data on the screen is not significantly changed.

What happens is actually the reverse of expected:
You see the pixelation more clearly. (unless they blur it with lenses etc)

Therefore, a low resolution VR image will likely not be satisfying.
